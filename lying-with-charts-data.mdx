A supplier fits an energy saving device to your cold store and sends a chart one month later. The bar for "before" is tall. The bar for "after" is a thin strip at the bottom. The invoice is 4,000 USD. The numbers under the bars are 12,400 kWh and 12,030 kWh: a fall of 3%. Nothing on the chart is false, and it still misleads. Darrell Huff's 1954 book "How to Lie with Statistics" first collected these tricks. This lesson shows the seven you will meet most often, how to read past each one, and how to draw your own data so that the picture and the numbers say the same thing. #DataVisualization #CriticalThinking #HonestData

## Trick 1: The Truncated Y-Axis

The y-axis is the vertical scale of a chart. A truncated axis is one that does not start at zero. It is the most common trick, because it makes a small difference look very large.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="The Deception">
    Take the supplier's chart step by step.

    1. The real change. Before: 12,400 kWh a month. After: 12,030 kWh. The fall is 370 kWh, and $370 / 12{,}400 = 3.0\%$.
    2. The supplier's axis starts at 12,000 kWh. The chart shows only the part of each bar above 12,000: 400 kWh of the first bar and 30 kWh of the second.
    3. What the eye compares. $400 / 30 \approx 13$. The first bar looks 13 times taller, so the chart looks like a fall of more than 90%.

    ```text
     Axis from 12,000 kWh       Axis from 0 kWh

     12,400 | ███               12,000 | ███   ███
     12,300 | ███                9,000 | ███   ███
     12,200 | ███                6,000 | ███   ███
     12,100 | ███                3,000 | ███   ███
     12,000 | ███   ▄▄▄              0 | ███   ███
             before after              before after
     "Use fell like a cliff."   "Almost the same."
    ```

    The honest version starts at zero, where the two bars are almost equal, and states the change in words: "3% less, 370 kWh a month". It also shows several months before the device, because a month 3% lower than the one before may be only the weather.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="When Truncation Is Legitimate">
    A cut axis is sometimes correct. A cold room that moves between 2 and 6 °C would be a flat line on an axis from 0 to 100 °C, and the variation that matters would be hidden. The rule is to be open about it:

    - Mark the axis clearly, so that the reader sees it does not start at zero.
    - Draw a break mark (a zigzag) on the axis.
    - Use a line or points, not bars. The height of a bar is read as the whole amount, and a cut axis makes that reading false.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="How to Spot It">
    Read the numbers on the y-axis before you look at the bars. If the axis does not start at zero, ask what the chart would look like if it did, and calculate the change in percent yourself.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

The following code plots the same sales data two ways. The first chart starts the y-axis at zero and shows growth of 6% over six months honestly. The second chart starts at 990, and the last bar is 7 times taller than the first.

```python title="honest_vs_misleading_chart.py"
import numpy as np
import matplotlib
matplotlib.use('Agg')
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

months = ['Jan', 'Feb', 'Mar', 'Apr', 'May', 'Jun']
sales = [1000, 1020, 1015, 1040, 1035, 1060]

fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(12, 5))

# Honest chart: y-axis starts at 0
ax1.bar(months, sales, color='steelblue')
ax1.set_ylim(0, 1200)
ax1.set_title('Honest Chart (y-axis from 0)')
ax1.set_ylabel('Monthly Sales (units)')

# Misleading chart: y-axis starts at 990
ax2.bar(months, sales, color='tomato')
ax2.set_ylim(990, 1070)
ax2.set_title('Misleading Chart (y-axis from 990)')
ax2.set_ylabel('Monthly Sales (units)')

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('honest_vs_misleading.png', dpi=150)
print("Saved: honest_vs_misleading.png")

actual_growth = 100 * (sales[-1] - sales[0]) / sales[0]
visual_ratio = (sales[-1] - 990) / (sales[0] - 990)
print(f"\nActual growth from Jan to Jun: {actual_growth:.1f}%")
print(f"Visual bar height ratio (misleading chart): {visual_ratio:.1f}x")
print(f"The misleading chart makes a {actual_growth:.1f}% increase look like a {visual_ratio:.1f}x increase.")
```

## Trick 2: Cherry-Picked Time Windows

<InArticleAd />


A time window is the period that a chart shows. By choosing where it starts and ends, the same data can be made to show growth, decline or no change.

<Card title="The Peak-to-Trough Trick" icon="warning">
Take monthly energy use that goes up and down around a level average. A chart from a low month to a high month shows strong growth. A chart from a high month to a low month shows a fall. The data are the same, and the window decides the story.

```text
Full data (18 months, level with ups and downs):
    ╭─╮   ╭─╮   ╭─╮   ╭─╮   ╭─╮   ╭─╮
───╯   ╰─╯   ╰─╯   ╰─╯   ╰─╯   ╰─╯   ╰───

Months 3 to 9 only (low to high):
        ╭─╮   ╭─╮
    ───╯   ╰─╯       "Growth!"

Months 6 to 12 only (high to low):
    ╭─╮
        ╰─╯   ╰───   "Decline!"
```
</Card>

### Engineering Examples

- **An energy saving.** "The cold store used 18% less after our service." The month before was the hottest of the year. Against the same month one year earlier, the saving is 2%.
- **Uptime.** "The generator was available 99.9% of this month." The three day failure was last month, outside the window.
- **Response time.** "Our service answered 30% faster this quarter." The quarter before had an unusual fault that made it slow.

**Counter:** Ask for the full history. If a chart shows one month, ask for the year. A fair comparison for anything that follows the seasons is the same period one year earlier.

## Trick 3: Misleading Scales

<InArticleAd />


### Logarithmic vs. Linear

On a linear axis, equal distances mean equal amounts: 0, 250, 500, 750. On a logarithmic (log) axis, equal distances mean equal factors: 1, 10, 100, 1,000. A log axis is useful when the data cover a very wide range. It misleads a reader who expects a linear axis.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="The Deception">
    On a log axis, the step from 100 to 1,000 has the same height as the step from 1,000 to 10,000. A reader who does not see that the axis is logarithmic takes a gain of 900 and a gain of 9,000 for the same thing. In the other direction, a linear axis makes growth that doubles every year look flat in the early years.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="When Each Scale Is Appropriate">
    **Use a log axis when:**
    - The data cover a range of 100 times or more
    - Changes in percent matter more than changes in amount

    **Use a linear axis when:**
    - The data cover a narrow range
    - Your readers may not know log axes
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="How to Spot It">
    Read the numbers on the axis. If they are 1, 10, 100, 1000, the axis is logarithmic. If they are 0, 250, 500, 750, 1000, it is linear.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

### Inconsistent Axis Intervals

The labels under a chart read "Jan, Feb, Mar, Jun, Dec", drawn at equal distances. The months with poor results are left out, and the unequal gaps change the slope of every trend. Check that equal distances along an axis are equal times.

## Trick 4: 3D Pie Charts (and Pie Charts in General)

<InArticleAd />


<Card title="Why 3D Pie Charts Are Always Wrong" icon="warning">
A 3D pie chart is drawn as a tilted disc. The slices at the front look larger than slices of the same value at the back, so a fair comparison is not possible.

Flat pie charts are also weak for comparison. People judge length (bars) and position (points) much more accurately than angle or area.

```text
  What the data says:         What the 3D chart implies:
  A: 30%  B: 25%              A looks ~35% (front, enlarged)
  C: 25%  D: 20%              D looks ~12% (back, compressed)
```

**Rule:** Never use a 3D pie chart. To show the parts of a whole, use a stacked bar, or a flat pie chart with the percentages written on it.
</Card>

## Trick 5: Dual Y-Axes

<InArticleAd />


A chart with two y-axes draws two different quantities, one against the left scale and one against the right. Because the two scales are chosen separately, the person who draws the chart decides how closely the two lines appear to follow each other.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="The Deception">
    A maintenance contractor draws "service visits each month" against the left axis and "plant output" against the right. The two lines rise together, and the chart suggests that more visits produce more output. But the range and the starting point of each axis were chosen to make the lines fit. Any two quantities that both rise over a year can be made to fit in this way.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="The Deeper Problem">
    By changing the range or the direction of one axis, the same two quantities can be made to move together, to move in opposite directions, or to show no relation. The reader cannot judge the real relation from such a chart.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="What to Do Instead">
    - Draw two charts, one above the other, with the same time axis.
    - If you want to compare the two changes, draw both as percent change from the same starting month.
    - Calculate the correlation and report the number.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Trick 6: Pictogram Abuse

<InArticleAd />


A pictogram shows an amount with a small picture, such as a barrel or a pump. It misleads when the picture is made larger to show a larger amount.

<Card title="The Height vs. Area Problem" icon="setting">
Site A produces 100 units and site B produces 200. The chart draws B twice as tall and, to keep its shape, twice as wide: four times the area of A. The eye sees four times, and the data say two times.

```text
Data: A = 100, B = 200 (2x ratio)

Wrong (scale height and width):
  A:  ▪         B:  ▪▪
                    ▪▪
  Visual area ratio: 4x (misleading)

Right (use equal-sized icons, vary quantity):
  A: ▪▪▪▪▪       B: ▪▪▪▪▪▪▪▪▪▪
  Visual ratio: 2x (accurate)
```

**Rule:** Show a larger amount with more pictures of the same size, not with a larger picture. Or use a bar chart.
</Card>

## Trick 7: Simpson's Paradox

<InArticleAd />


Simpson's paradox is a result that holds inside every group and reverses when the groups are added together. It is a property of the numbers, not of the drawing, but a chart of totals alone can use it.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Engineering Example">
    Two contractors repair pumps for you. You measure the average time of a repair:

    | Contractor | Small faults | Large faults | Average of all |
    |------|-----------|-----------|----------------|
    | Contractor A | 2 hours (80 jobs) | 8 hours (20 jobs) | 3.2 hours |
    | Contractor B | 1 hour (10 jobs) | 6 hours (90 jobs) | 5.5 hours |

    Contractor A has the lower average: 3.2 hours against 5.5. But contractor B is faster at both kinds of work: 1 hour against 2 for small faults, and 6 hours against 8 for large faults. B's average is higher only because 90 of B's 100 jobs were large faults: $(1 \times 10 + 6 \times 90) / 100 = 5.5$.

    A bar chart of the two averages says "choose A". A chart with the two kinds of fault side by side says "choose B". Both are drawn from true numbers.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="How to Handle It">
    When you see one number for each of several groups, ask whether the groups did the same mix of work. If not, compare like with like: small with small, large with large. In your own reports, show the total and the parts, and explain any difference between them.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

## How to Present Your Own Data Honestly

<InArticleAd />


<Steps>
1. **Start the y-axis of a bar chart at zero.** To show detail in a narrow range, use a line or points and mark the break in the axis.

2. **Show the full time range.** If you point to one period, show enough before and after it for the reader to judge whether it is typical.

3. **Label everything.** Names of the axes, units, the number of measurements and the source of the data belong on the chart.

4. **Choose the chart for the question.** Bars to compare amounts, a line for change over time, points for the relation between two quantities. No 3D.

5. **Show the uncertainty.** Add error bars, or at least the number of measurements. A mean with no measure of scatter is incomplete.

6. **Do not use two y-axes.** Draw two charts, one above the other, with the same time axis.

7. **Show the single measurements when you can.** Points for every reading tell more than one bar for their mean.

8. **Use colour and size with care.** Colour separates groups that mean something. Size grows in the same proportion as the value.
</Steps>

:::tip[Instrument it]
A monitoring screen, like the live ones at [siliconwit.io/public-devices](https://siliconwit.io/public-devices), is charts of sensor data, so every trick in this lesson can mislead the operator who trusts it. A cut y-axis turns normal drift into a crisis, a smoothed line hides the spike that was the real fault, and two axes invent a relation that two sensors do not have. When you show live data to someone who will act on it, show the zero, keep the raw line next to the smoothed one, and show the uncertainty.
:::

## A Checklist for Reading Charts

<InArticleAd />


<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Check the axes" icon="star">
    Does the y-axis start at zero? If not, is the cut marked? Are equal distances equal amounts, or is the axis logarithmic?
  </Card>

  <Card title="Check the time window" icon="setting">
    What happened before and after the period shown? Would a different start or end change the trend?
  </Card>

  <Card title="Check the comparison" icon="approve-check">
    Did the groups do the same mix of work? Are the numbers of measurements similar? Could the result reverse inside the groups (Simpson's paradox)?
  </Card>

  <Card title="Check the drawing" icon="warning">
    Do heights and areas grow in proportion to the values? Is there a 3D effect? Would a plain bar or line show the same data more truthfully?
  </Card>
</CardGrid>

## Seen in the field

<InArticleAd />


A window that is shorter than the trend is the most common way a chart misleads, and nobody intends it. A district meter looked healthy on any three day view for nine weeks, because a fault that grows by 0.3% a day cannot appear in three days of data. Ten weeks of the same readings show it at once, in [nine weeks of a leak growing](/blog/remote-monitoring/minimum-night-flow-leak-detection/).
