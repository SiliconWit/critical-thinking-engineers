A generator has cost 6,000 USD in repairs in two years, and it needs another repair. "We cannot scrap it now, after all we have spent on it." Most people find that sentence reasonable, and it is an error: the money already spent cannot be recovered by any choice you make today. An error that most minds make in the same direction, again and again, is called a cognitive bias. This lesson shows the biases that cost the most in technical work, with a counter for each. #CognitiveBias #EngineeringDecisions #CriticalThinking

| Bias | What happens | What it costs |
|------|--------------|---------------|
| Confirmation bias | You look for what you expect | The real cause is missed |
| Survivorship bias | You study only what survived | Wrong lessons |
| Sunk cost fallacy | Past spending decides the future | Money follows a bad choice |
| Dunning-Kruger effect | Little knowledge, much confidence | Risks nobody saw |
| Hindsight bias | "I knew it all along" | Nothing is learned |
| Bandwagon effect | You follow the crowd | Copied bad choices |

## Confirmation Bias

**What it is:** The habit of looking for, noticing and remembering information that agrees with what you already believe, and of passing over information that disagrees.

Engineering work is the testing of ideas against reality. Confirmation bias damages exactly that step, because you choose the tests.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="In Fault Finding">
    A pump trips now and then. You suspect the variable speed drive, so you open its fault history, and you find some overcurrent entries. You declare the drive guilty and order a new one. The trips continue. The real cause was a float switch with a loose terminal, and the overcurrent entries were old and harmless. You found what you looked for because you looked only where you expected to find it.

    **The fix:** Before you test your first suspect, write down at least three other causes. Test the one you think least likely first. This makes you collect evidence that could prove you wrong.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="In Testing">
    You wrote a program and you are proud of it. Without noticing, you test it with the cases where it works, normal inputs in the expected range, and stay away from the unusual cases that could show a weakness.

    **The fix:** Let a person who did not write the code write the tests, or ask yourself "how would I break this?"
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="In Design Reviews">
    You proposed replacing the old compressor. In the review you listen closely to every comment in favor, and you discount the concern that the new unit needs a larger supply cable. You think "they do not understand the benefits" and you do not examine the concern.

    **The fix:** Give one person in every review the job of arguing against the proposal as strongly as they can. Then the search for opposing evidence is part of the process and does not depend on your mood.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Survivorship Bias

<InArticleAd />


**What it is:** Drawing a conclusion only from the cases that passed some filter, and ignoring the cases that did not pass.

<Card title="Abraham Wald and the Missing Bullet Holes" icon="star">
During World War II, the US military marked where returning bombers had been hit, and planned to add armor where the holes were most frequent. The mathematician Abraham Wald pointed out the error: they saw only the planes that came back. Their holes showed where a bomber could be hit and still fly. The places with no holes were where a hit was fatal, and those were the places to protect.

The missing data (the planes that were lost) told a different story from the visible data (the planes that returned).
</Card>

The program below repeats Wald's situation. Each of 1,000 planes may be hit in any of 5 zones, and a plane hit in the engine does not return. Count the hits on the planes that return and the engine shows none, although it is the zone that most needs armor.

```python title="survivorship_bias_simulation.py"
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(42)
n_planes = 1000
zones = ['Fuselage', 'Wings', 'Tail', 'Cockpit', 'Engine']
n_zones = len(zones)

# Each plane gets random hits (0 or 1) in each zone
hit_probability = 0.3
hits = np.random.random((n_planes, n_zones)) < hit_probability

# Planes hit in the engine (zone index 4) are destroyed
engine_hit = hits[:, 4]
survived = ~engine_hit
n_survived = np.sum(survived)
n_destroyed = np.sum(engine_hit)

# Analysts only see the surviving planes
surviving_hits = hits[survived]
hit_counts = np.sum(surviving_hits, axis=0)

print(f"Total planes sent: {n_planes}")
print(f"Survived: {n_survived}  |  Destroyed (engine hit): {n_destroyed}\n")
print("Hit counts on SURVIVING planes (what analysts see):")
for zone, count in zip(zones, hit_counts):
    bar = '#' * (count // 5)
    print(f"  {zone:10s}: {count:3d} hits  {bar}")

print(f"\nNaive conclusion: 'Armor the fuselage, wings and tail, they take the most hits!'")
print(f"Wald's insight:   'The engine has ZERO hits on survivors because engine hits")
print(f"                   are FATAL. Armor the engine.'")
print(f"\nThe missing data (destroyed planes) tells the real story.")
```

### Survivorship Bias in Engineering

| Scenario | What You See | What You Miss |
|----------|-------------|---------------|
| A supplier's reference sites | "These 12 sites have run our controller for five years without a failure" | How many controllers were sold five years ago, and what happened at the other sites |
| Old machines | "They built them better then. Look at this 40 year old motor" | The many motors of the same age that were scrapped long ago |
| A brand you know | "We have used this pump brand for years with no problems" | The jobs where it gave trouble and was quietly replaced |
| Popular software | "This framework has millions of downloads, so it must be good" | The people who tried it, met problems and moved to something else without a word |

### How to Counter Survivorship Bias

<Steps>
1. **Ask: "Where are the failures?"** When someone offers success stories as evidence, ask what happened to the cases that did not succeed. If nobody knows, the success stories are weak evidence.

2. **Ask for the starting number.** "12 sites without a failure" means one thing out of 13 installed and another out of 60.

3. **Collect failure records on purpose.** Read reports of what went wrong, and talk to the sites that stopped using the product, not only the ones on the reference list.
</Steps>

## Sunk Cost Fallacy

<InArticleAd />


**What it is:** Continuing to put money or effort into a choice because of what has already been spent on it, and not because of what the next spending will buy.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Worked Case">
    A site's generator has cost 6,000 USD in repairs in two years. It needs another repair of 2,500 USD now. The technician expects it to go on costing about 3,000 USD a year in repairs and extra fuel. A new generator costs 9,000 USD and should cost about 500 USD a year to maintain.

    The manager says: "We have put 6,000 USD into this machine. If we replace it, that money is wasted."

    Compare only what each choice costs from today, over the next three years:

    | Choice | Cost from today |
    |--------|-----------------|
    | Keep and repair | 2,500 + 3 × 3,000 = 11,500 USD |
    | Replace | 9,000 + 3 × 500 = 10,500 USD |

    Replacing is 1,000 USD cheaper. The 6,000 USD appears nowhere in the table: it is spent under both choices, so it cannot make one better than the other. With other numbers the repair could win, and the method stays the same: only future costs enter.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Why It Is So Powerful">
    Giving up past work feels like admitting a mistake. The Concorde supersonic airliner is the example most often given: the British and French governments went on paying for it long after it was clear that it would never earn its cost, because too much had been spent to "waste".
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="How to Counter">
    Use the clean slate test: "If we were starting today, with what we know now, would we choose this?" If the answer is no, the past spending does not change it. Some organizations write "stop conditions" at the start of a project: the results at which the project will be ended, agreed before anyone is attached to it.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Dunning-Kruger Effect

<InArticleAd />


**What it is:** People with little skill in a field tend to rate their own ability too high, and people with great skill tend to rate theirs a little too low.

<Card title="The Confidence-Competence Curve" icon="warning">
When you first learn a subject, it looks simple. You finish a short course, complete one small job and feel confident. Popular drawings of the Dunning-Kruger effect call this the "peak of Mount Stupid". With more experience you find the difficulties you did not know were there, and your confidence falls. With real expertise it returns, and this time it rests on understanding.
</Card>

### How It Shows Up in Engineering

| Stage | What the person says | What is behind it | Danger |
|-------|-----------|------------|--------|
| Beginner | "I watched a video on drive settings. I can set up any drive" | One short course | Promises too much, skips the safe procedure |
| Intermediate | "The more I learn, the less I feel I know" | Several jobs completed | Stays silent in meetings although the knowledge is real |
| Expert | "I know what I know and what I do not know" | Years of deep experience | Assumes everyone else knows it too |

In a meeting, the person who knows least is often the most confident, and confidence moves decisions.

**Counter:** Judge a proposal on its evidence and its details, not on how sure the speaker sounds. Ask for a real example, for what happens in the unusual case, and for how it could fail. A confident claim that cannot answer exact questions is a warning sign.

## Hindsight Bias

<InArticleAd />


**What it is:** After a result is known, believing that you "knew it all along", or that anyone could have predicted it.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Engineering Example">
    A cold room compressor fails on a hot weekend, and the stock is lost. In the review, someone says: "That contactor has been noisy for months. It was obvious." Before the failure, nobody had reported the contactor, and it was one of perhaps a hundred small defects on the site. It looks obvious only after the failure.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Why It Matters">
    If the team believes the failure was obvious, it blames the people who missed it and leaves the system unchanged. The lesson becomes "someone should have been cleverer", when it should be "we need a temperature alarm that reaches a person, and a defect list that someone reads".
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="How to Counter">
    In every review, ask: "With what we knew then, not what we know now, was the decision reasonable?" Then improve the system (alarms, inspections, a spare unit) and do not blame a person for failing to see the future.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Bandwagon Effect

<InArticleAd />


**What it is:** Taking up a belief, a product or a method because many others have done so, without judging it yourself.

<Card title="Technology Hype Cycles" icon="setting">
Every few years one technology becomes the answer to every problem. At the height of the fashion, people adopt it because "everyone is doing it" and they fear being left behind, not because they checked that it fits.

A variable speed drive is a good example. On a pump whose flow must change through the day it can save a lot of energy. On a pump that only lifts water to a tank at one steady flow, it has nothing to adjust, and the drive's own losses mean the pump uses a little more energy than before. "Every site is fitting them" does not tell you which of the two pumps you have.
</Card>

**Counter:** For each choice of technology, write the answer to one question: "Why is this the right tool for our problem, our conditions and our people?" If the answer comes down to "it is popular", it is a bandwagon argument, not a technical one.

## Availability Heuristic and Anchoring (Revisited)

<InArticleAd />


Availability (judging by what comes to mind most easily) and anchoring (being pulled by the first number) were introduced with System 1. In an organization, each has a standard fix.

**Availability in risk assessment.** After a theft, security fills every meeting for months, while corroded connectors and failing power supplies, which have caused no trouble lately, are forgotten. The fix is a risk register: a written list of risks, reviewed on fixed dates and updated from records, not from memory.

**Anchoring in estimates.** If a manager says "about a week" before the team has estimated, the team's number moves toward one week. The fix is for everyone to write an estimate before anyone speaks. A wide spread is useful: some people see work that others do not.

## Case Study: Cognitive Bias and the Space Shuttle Disasters

<InArticleAd />


The losses of the space shuttles Challenger and Columbia show the same biases at work in a whole organization.

<Tabs>
  <TabItem label="Challenger (1986)">
    **Confirmation bias:** The O-rings are rubber seals in the joints of the booster rockets. Engineers had data showing that the seals were eroded on cold launches. But they analyzed only the launches where erosion was seen, not the launches where it was not. When all the launches were examined together, the link between cold and seal damage was clear.

    **Normalization of deviance:** Erosion had happened on earlier flights without a disaster. Each such flight made the risk seem smaller, although the specification allowed no erosion at all. Repeated "success" outside the specification produced a false feeling of safety.

    **Groupthink and authority pressure:** Engineers at Morton Thiokol, the maker of the boosters, recommended against launching in the cold. Under pressure from NASA management, the recommendation was reversed. The burden of proof had been turned around: the engineers were asked to prove that the launch was unsafe, when the normal rule is to prove that it is safe.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem label="Columbia (2003)">
    **WYSIATI (What You See Is All There Is):** Foam had struck the shuttle's heat protection on earlier missions. Engineers asked for satellite pictures to assess the damage to Columbia's wing. Management refused, partly because foam strikes were classed as a maintenance matter and not as a danger to the flight. Without the pictures, everyone worked from incomplete information and assumed the best.

    **Anchoring:** Earlier foam strikes had caused no disaster. That history ruled the risk assessment, although the strike on Columbia was much larger than the earlier ones.

    **Hindsight:** After the disaster it seemed "obvious" that foam hitting the front edge of the wing at hundreds of miles per hour could be fatal (the strike broke a reinforced carbon-carbon panel, not a tile). Before the disaster it was classed as routine maintenance.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

In both cases the biases were built into the way the organization classed risks and made decisions. Asking individuals to be more careful would not have been enough.

## Debiasing Strategies for Engineering Teams

<InArticleAd />


A fixed practice is more reliable than good intentions, because it does not depend on a person remembering to doubt at the right moment.

<CardGrid>
  <Card title="Pre-mortem Analysis" icon="star">
    Before you commit to a plan, imagine that a year has passed and the project has failed. Each person writes down, alone, why it failed. This brings out risks that the group would not say aloud. Research on "prospective hindsight" (Mitchell, Russo and Pennington, 1989), the idea behind the pre-mortem, found that imagining a result as already having happened increases the ability to identify reasons for it by 30%.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Red Team / Blue Team" icon="setting">
    One team argues against the proposal as strongly as it can, and its job is to find faults, not to be fair. A second team defends. The people who decide hear both.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Checklists" icon="warning">
    Aviation learned that a checklist stops an experienced pilot from leaving out a step that "feels" unnecessary. A checklist for commissioning, for a shutdown or for a design review does the same: the critical steps are written down and do not rest on memory.
  </Card>
</CardGrid>
