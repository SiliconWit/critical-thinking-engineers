A standby generator is approved with a fuel budget of 150 USD a month. The first invoice is 430 USD. Nobody lied. Somebody multiplied four guesses, wrote down one number and gave no range, and the reader took the number as a fact. Every number in engineering carries doubt: a meter has a tolerance, a count of outage hours is a memory, a price moves. This lesson shows how to make a fast estimate, how to state the range that belongs to it, and how doubt in the inputs becomes doubt in the result. #Estimation #Uncertainty #CriticalThinking

## Fermi Estimation: Good Enough in 60 Seconds

Often you do not need an exact answer. You need to know whether the answer is near 10, near 1,000 or near a million. The physicist Enrico Fermi was known for answering such questions with a chain of rough guesses and round numbers, so the method carries his name.

### A worked case: diesel for a standby generator

How much will the diesel cost each month for a generator that covers power cuts at a small factory? Nobody has measured it yet. Break the question into factors you can guess, and give each one a best value, a low value and a high value.

| Factor | Low | Best | High |
|--------|-----|------|------|
| Hours of power cut in a month | 20 | 40 | 80 |
| Average load on the generator, kW | 20 | 30 | 40 |
| Diesel for each kWh, litres | 0.25 | 0.30 | 0.40 |
| Price of a litre, USD | 1.00 | 1.20 | 1.50 |

<Steps>
1. **Multiply the best values.** $40 \times 30 = 1{,}200$ kWh a month. $1{,}200 \times 0.30 = 360$ litres. $360 \times 1.20 = 432$, so about 430 USD a month.

2. **Find the outer limits.** All low: $20 \times 20 \times 0.25 \times 1.00 = 100$ USD. All high: $80 \times 40 \times 0.40 \times 1.50 = 1{,}920$ USD. The true value is almost surely inside, but this range is too wide to use, because it is unlikely that all four guesses are wrong in the same direction.

3. **Find the likely range.** The hours may be wrong by a factor of 2 in each direction. The other three factors are known within a factor of 1.2 to 1.4. When errors are independent, they partly cancel, and the result is wrong by little more than its worst factor: here about 2.3. So the likely range is $432 / 2.3$ to $432 \times 2.3$, about 190 to 1,000 USD.

4. **State the answer with its range.** "About 430 USD a month, likely between 190 and 1,000." A budget of 150 USD is outside the likely range, and you know that before the first invoice.
</Steps>

Step 3 also shows where to spend effort. The exact fuel price would hardly narrow the range. One month of logged outage hours would cut it to about 260 to 710 USD.

### Practice: Estimate Before You Calculate

Before you use a formula or a spreadsheet, take 30 seconds to estimate the answer with every value rounded to one digit. If the detailed result is far from the estimate, one of them is wrong. It is usually the detailed one: a typing error, a wrong unit or a wrong formula.

## Error Bars: What They Actually Mean

<InArticleAd />


An error bar is a line drawn through a point on a chart. It shows the range in which the true value probably lies. A cold room that used "52 kWh a day, plus or minus 3" is drawn as a point at 52 with a bar from 49 to 55.

Most readers compare the points and do not look at the bars. That is how a saving is "proved" that is not there. Suppose a new door seal is fitted, and the daily energy is measured before (A) and after (B):

```text
 Bars overlap: a saving is not shown

    46      49      52      55   kWh a day
            |------ A ------|
    |------ B ------|

 Bars apart: the saving is real

    45  46  47          51  52  53
                        |-- A --|
    |-- B --|
```

### Overlapping Error Bars

If the bars of two values overlap, you cannot say with confidence that the values differ. Before, $52 \pm 3$ kWh a day. After, $49 \pm 3$ kWh a day. A reaches down to 49 and B reaches up to 52. A claim of "3 kWh a day saved" is not supported by these measurements. Measure for longer, so that the bars become shorter.

### Non-overlapping Error Bars

If there is a clear gap between the bars, the evidence is much stronger. Before, $52 \pm 1$ kWh a day. After, $46 \pm 1$ kWh a day. The gap between 47 and 51 is larger than either bar.

### Common Mistakes with Error Bars

| Mistake | Why it matters |
|---------|---------------|
| Not looking at the bars | You treat uncertain data as exact |
| Assuming what the bars mean | They may show the scatter of the readings, the uncertainty of the mean, a 95% interval, or the lowest and highest value |
| Drawing bars and not saying what they show | The reader cannot use them |

## Significant Figures: Your ADC Is Lying to You

<InArticleAd />


Significant figures are the digits of a number that carry real information. An ADC (analog to digital converter) is the chip that turns a voltage into a whole number. A 10-bit ADC with a 3.3 V reference gives numbers from 0 to 1023. A reading of 1023 means 3.3 V. What does a reading of 512 mean?

```
voltage = 512 * 3.3 / 1023 = 1.65161...
```

The calculator gives many decimal places. The ADC cannot tell apart two voltages that are closer than one step, and one step is $3.3 / 1024 \approx 3.2$ mV. So the digits after the third decimal place carry no information. The honest value is 1.652 V, or 1.65 V.

### Rules of Thumb for Significant Figures

<Card title="Multiplication and Division" icon="puzzle">
Give the result as many significant figures as the input that has the fewest. If $R = 4.7$ kohm (2 figures) and $I = 0.0023$ A (2 figures), report $V = R \times I$ as 11 V, not 10.81 V.
</Card>

<Card title="Addition and Subtraction" icon="puzzle">
Give the result as many decimal places as the input that has the fewest. If you measure 12.3 V and subtract 0.057 V, report 12.2 V, not 12.243 V.
</Card>

### The False Precision Trap

False precision means reporting more digits than the measurement supports. It looks careful, and it tells the reader you are more certain than you are.

A 5,000 litre tank fills in 37 minutes, timed on a watch to the nearest minute. The division gives $5{,}000 / 37 = 135.135\ldots$ litres a minute. The time has two significant figures, so the honest flow is 140 litres a minute, or "about 135". A report that says 135.135 invites the reader to compare it with a pump curve to the last digit.

### Practical Significance vs. Numerical Precision

A second question follows: do the digits you have matter for the decision?

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Unnecessary Precision">
"The cold room sensor reads 3.4872 °C. The setpoint is 3.5 °C. The error is 0.0128 °C."

The thermostat switches only when the temperature moves 0.5 °C from the setpoint. It will never act on the fourth decimal place.
</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Appropriate Precision">
"The cold room sensor reads 3.5 °C. The setpoint is 3.5 °C. Within the limit."

This is all the operator needs.
</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Propagation of Uncertainty

<InArticleAd />


When you put uncertain values into a formula, the result is uncertain too. Propagation of uncertainty is the calculation of how much.

The example is a resistor and a capacitor that form a filter. Its time constant $\tau = RC$ is the time the filter takes to respond. The resistor is $R = 10 \pm 0.5$ kohm (5% tolerance) and the capacitor is $C = 100 \pm 10$ nF (10% tolerance). How uncertain is $\tau$?

### The Simple Rule for Products

When values are multiplied or divided, work with relative uncertainties, which are the uncertainties as fractions of the values. Square each one, add the squares and take the square root. This is called adding in quadrature. It gives less than a plain sum, because two independent errors seldom reach their limits in the same direction together. Here $\delta$ means "the uncertainty of":

$$
\frac{\delta \tau}{\tau} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\delta R}{R}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\delta C}{C}\right)^2}
$$

$$
\frac{\delta \tau}{\tau} = \sqrt{(0.05)^2 + (0.10)^2} = \sqrt{0.0025 + 0.01} = \sqrt{0.0125} \approx 0.112
$$

So $\tau = RC = 10\text{k} \times 100\text{n} = 1.0$ ms with about 11.2% uncertainty: $\tau = 1.0 \pm 0.11$ ms. This is the same rule that gave the likely range of the diesel estimate.

Note what kind of number 11.2% is. The inputs are tolerance limits, so it is a combined limit that nearly all parts will meet. It is not a standard deviation, the usual measure of scatter. If a part is equally likely to have any value inside its tolerance band of half-width $a$, its standard deviation is $a/\sqrt{3}$, so the standard deviation of $\tau$ is $11.2\% / \sqrt{3} \approx 6.5\%$.

The program below checks the formula by trial. It builds 100,000 imaginary filters, each with a resistor and a capacitor drawn at random from inside their tolerance bands (a Monte Carlo simulation), and measures the scatter of $\tau$. The simulated standard deviation is 0.0645 ms, which matches 6.5%, and 95% of the values fall between 0.885 ms and 1.121 ms, close to the 11.2% limit.

```python title="error_propagation_monte_carlo.py"
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(42)
n_samples = 100000

# Component nominal values and tolerances
R_nom = 10e3      # 10 kohm
R_tol = 0.05      # 5%
C_nom = 100e-9    # 100 nF
C_tol = 0.10      # 10%

# Monte Carlo: sample from uniform distributions within tolerance bands
R_samples = R_nom * (1 + R_tol * np.random.uniform(-1, 1, n_samples))
C_samples = C_nom * (1 + C_tol * np.random.uniform(-1, 1, n_samples))
tau_samples = R_samples * C_samples

# Monte Carlo statistics
tau_mean = np.mean(tau_samples)
tau_std = np.std(tau_samples)
tau_lo, tau_hi = np.percentile(tau_samples, [2.5, 97.5])

# Analytical propagation (quadrature of relative uncertainties).
# A tolerance is a limit, not a standard deviation. For a uniform
# distribution of half-width a, the standard deviation is a / sqrt(3).
tau_nominal = R_nom * C_nom
rel_limit = np.sqrt(R_tol**2 + C_tol**2)   # combined tolerance limit
rel_unc = rel_limit / np.sqrt(3)           # combined standard deviation
tau_analytical_std = tau_nominal * rel_unc

print(f"R = {R_nom/1e3:.0f} kohm +/- {R_tol*100:.0f}%,  C = {C_nom*1e9:.0f} nF +/- {C_tol*100:.0f}%")
print(f"Nominal tau = RC = {tau_nominal*1e3:.2f} ms\n")
print(f"Monte Carlo ({n_samples} samples):")
print(f"  Mean:    {tau_mean*1e3:.4f} ms")
print(f"  Std:     {tau_std*1e3:.4f} ms")
print(f"  95% CI:  [{tau_lo*1e3:.4f}, {tau_hi*1e3:.4f}] ms\n")
print(f"Analytical propagation:")
print(f"  Limit:   {tau_nominal*rel_limit*1e3:.4f} ms  (relative = {rel_limit*100:.1f}%)")
print(f"  Std:     {tau_analytical_std*1e3:.4f} ms  (relative = {rel_unc*100:.1f}%)")
print(f"\nThe Monte Carlo and analytical standard deviations agree closely.")
print(f"Your 1.0 ms time constant is really 1.0 +/- {tau_std*1e3:.2f} ms (one standard deviation).")
```

### The Simple Rule for Sums

When values are added or subtracted, the absolute uncertainties (in the unit of the value, not in percent) add in quadrature:

$$
\delta(A + B) = \sqrt{(\delta A)^2 + (\delta B)^2}
$$

This rule has a hard consequence for any loss found by subtraction. A water meter at the supply reads $120.0 \pm 2.4$ m³ for a day. The customer meters add up to $111.0 \pm 2.2$ m³. The loss is the difference:

$$
120.0 - 111.0 = 9.0 \pm \sqrt{2.4^2 + 2.2^2} = 9.0 \pm 3.3 \text{ m}^3
$$

Each meter is good to 2%, and the loss is known only to 36%. The difference of two large numbers that are close together is always much less certain than either number.

### Tolerance Stackup in Practice

If you stack five parts, each with a tolerance of $\pm 0.1$ mm, the worst case is $\pm 0.5$ mm: all five at their limit in the same direction. The statistical result is $\pm 0.1 \times \sqrt{5} \approx \pm 0.22$ mm. Designing to 0.22 mm when 0.5 mm is not needed saves money on every part.

### When to Use Worst-Case vs. Statistical Analysis

| Situation | Use |
|-----------|-----|
| A failure can injure people | Worst case |
| Few units, and each one must work | Worst case |
| Many units, and a rare failure is acceptable | Statistical |
| Early design, rough sizing | Statistical |
| Final check before release | Both, and compare |

## Confidence Intervals

<InArticleAd />


A report says $23.5 \pm 0.3$ °C. The $\pm 0.3$ may be the scatter of the readings, the lowest and highest reading, or a 95% confidence interval. These are different sizes, so a report must say which one it is.

### What a 95% Confidence Interval Means

A confidence interval is a range calculated from your readings by a fixed method. "95%" describes the method: if you repeated the whole measurement many times and calculated an interval each time, about 95 of every 100 intervals would contain the true value. It does not say that the true value has a 95% probability of being in this one interval, a difference that many experienced people miss.

### Computing a Confidence Interval

Suppose you measure a voltage 10 times and get these values:

| Trial | Voltage (V) |
|-------|-------------|
| 1 | 3.28 |
| 2 | 3.31 |
| 3 | 3.29 |
| 4 | 3.33 |
| 5 | 3.30 |
| 6 | 3.27 |
| 7 | 3.32 |
| 8 | 3.29 |
| 9 | 3.31 |
| 10 | 3.30 |

The mean is $\bar{x} = 3.300$ V. The standard deviation, which measures the scatter of single readings, is $s = 0.018$ V. The mean of 10 readings is steadier than one reading: its uncertainty, the standard error, is $s / \sqrt{n} = 0.018 / \sqrt{10} = 0.0057$ V.

To turn the standard error into a 95% interval, multiply it by a factor from the t table, which is larger when the readings are few. For 10 readings (9 degrees of freedom) it is about 2.26:

$$
3.300 \pm 2.26 \times 0.0057 = 3.300 \pm 0.013 \text{ V}
$$

Report it as: "The voltage is 3.300 V (95% confidence interval 3.287 to 3.313 V)."

### Measurement Uncertainty Budgets

An uncertainty budget is a table of every source of uncertainty in a measurement and the size of each. For a temperature measurement:

| Source | Type | Uncertainty (°C) |
|--------|------|-----------------|
| Sensor calibration | Systematic | 0.10 |
| ADC quantization | Systematic | 0.05 |
| Self-heating | Systematic | 0.02 |
| Noise (random) | Random | 0.08 |
| **Combined** | | **0.14** |

The combined value is the sum in quadrature: $\sqrt{0.10^2 + 0.05^2 + 0.02^2 + 0.08^2} = 0.14$ °C. The largest source decides most of the total, so that is the one to improve.

### Systematic vs. Random Uncertainty

The "Type" column separates two kinds of uncertainty that need different cures:

<CardGrid>
<Card title="Random (Type A)" icon="random">
Random uncertainty changes from one reading to the next, as electrical noise does. Averaging reduces it: the standard error falls as $1/\sqrt{n}$, so 100 readings give 10 times less random uncertainty than one.
</Card>
<Card title="Systematic (Type B)" icon="setting">
Systematic uncertainty is the same in every reading. A sensor that reads 0.2 °C too high does so each time, and averaging does not help. Calibration against a better instrument reduces it.
</Card>
</CardGrid>

One caution about the labels. In the formal guide to measurement uncertainty (the GUM), Type A and Type B describe how an uncertainty is evaluated: Type A from statistics on repeated readings, Type B from other information such as a datasheet or a calibration certificate. They often line up with random and systematic effects, as in the table above, but they are not the same classification.

Readings that agree closely with each other are precise. They are not necessarily accurate: all of them may be wrong by the same amount.

## Putting It All Together

<InArticleAd />


<Steps>
1. **Estimate first.** Before you measure or calculate, make a Fermi estimate with a range.
2. **Know the limits of your instrument.** Its resolution and its accuracy set the smallest uncertainty you can have.
3. **Report the right number of significant figures.** The digits must match what the measurement supports.
4. **Propagate the uncertainty.** If the inputs are uncertain, calculate how uncertain the result is.
5. **Say what your range means.** A standard deviation, a 95% confidence interval and a worst case limit are different things.
</Steps>

For every number, ask: **"How sure am I about this number?"** If you cannot give its range, you have a guess that looks exact.

<Card title="Think About It" icon="information">
A datasheet states a range for the same reason. A 10 kohm resistor with 1% tolerance is $10.0 \pm 0.1$ kohm. A 3.3 V regulator with 2% accuracy gives $3.3 \pm 0.066$ V. The maker promises the range, not the middle value.
</Card>

## Common Mistakes Engineers Make With Numbers

<InArticleAd />


### Treating Typical Values as Guaranteed

A datasheet often gives a minimum, a typical and a maximum value. The typical value was measured on a sample of parts in stated conditions. It is not a promise.

<Card title="Design to the Limits" icon="warning">
Design for the minimum and the maximum in the datasheet. The typical value tells you what is common. The limits tell you what you may receive. A pump chosen on its typical flow will disappoint on the day you receive one at the minimum.
</Card>

### Ignoring Temperature Effects

Values drift with temperature. A ceramic capacitor rated at 100 nF might actually be 80 nF at your operating temperature and DC bias voltage if it uses X5R dielectric. A crystal oscillator rated at 20 MHz might be 20.0004 MHz on a hot day. Put the temperature figures from the datasheet in your uncertainty budget.

### Confusing Resolution with Accuracy

Resolution is the smallest change an instrument can show. Accuracy is how close its reading is to the true value. A display that shows 4.37 °C has a resolution of 0.01 °C. If the datasheet says the accuracy is 0.5 °C, the true temperature is somewhere between 3.87 and 4.87 °C.

The same holds inside the instrument. A 16-bit ADC has 65,536 steps, but it also has offset error, gain error and noise. With 2 steps of noise and 5 steps of offset error, about 13 to 14 of its 16 bits are useful.

| Specification | What it means |
|---------------|---------------|
| Resolution | Smallest change you can detect |
| Accuracy | How close the reading is to the true value |
| Precision | How well repeated readings agree |
| Noise | Random variation in the reading |

High resolution with poor accuracy is a ruler with very fine marks that begins at 0.3 mm and not at 0. You can read it to 0.1 mm, and every reading is wrong by 0.3 mm.

### Forgetting About Units

The Mars Climate Orbiter was lost because one team used metric units and another used imperial units. The same error, on a smaller scale, happens whenever litres a minute meet cubic metres an hour in one spreadsheet.

Carry the units through every step of a calculation. If the units of the result are wrong, the calculation is wrong.

## How Much Effort to Spend on Uncertainty

<InArticleAd />


Ask one question first: would a different value inside the range change the decision?

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Quick Estimate Is Fine">
- A large margin. If the load needs 6 kW and the generator gives 20 kW, the load does not need a third digit.
- A choice that is cheap to change, such as an alarm limit.
- A planning talk. "About three months" is enough.
</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Full Analysis Needed">
- A claim of a saving. If a supplier's device is said to save 5% and your meter is good to 2%, the uncertainty decides whether the saving exists.
- Many units. A 1% failure rate on a million units is 10,000 failures.
- Safety: the load on a structure, the pressure in a vessel.
- Two options that are close. If one pump uses 50 kWh a day and another 55 kWh, you need the uncertainty to know whether the difference is real.
</TabItem>
</Tabs>

Precision that is not needed costs money and time. A 0.1% resistor where a 1% resistor would work costs more for no benefit. Match the effort to the decision.

## Exercises

<InArticleAd />


1. **Fermi estimation.** How many litres of water does your site use in a day? Break the question into at least four factors, give each a low, a best and a high value, and state your answer with a likely range. Then read the meter on two days and compare.

2. **Significant figures.** Your 12-bit ADC has a 5.0 V reference. You read a raw value of 2048. How many significant figures should you report in the computed voltage? What is the honest voltage value?

3. **Error propagation.** You are designing a voltage divider with $R_1 = 10 \pm 0.5$ kohm and $R_2 = 20 \pm 1.0$ kohm. The output voltage is $V_{out} = V_{in} \times R_2 / (R_1 + R_2)$. If $V_{in} = 5.00 \pm 0.05$ V, what is $V_{out}$ and its uncertainty?

4. **Confidence intervals.** You record the daily energy use of a cold room on 20 days and get a mean of 142 kWh with a standard deviation of 18 kWh. Compute the 95% confidence interval for the mean daily use. (Hint: the t-value for 19 degrees of freedom at 95% is about 2.09.)

## Summary

<InArticleAd />


Every number carries uncertainty, and a number without a range invites false confidence. A Fermi estimate gives a fast check and shows which factor to measure first. Significant figures stop you claiming precision you do not have. Propagation shows how uncertainties combine, and why a small difference of two large readings is weak evidence. A confidence interval states how sure you are, if you say what it is.

## Seen in the field

<InArticleAd />


A quantity that cannot be measured directly can often be bounded by measuring it when every other contribution is zero. A plant air compressor is a clear example. During production you cannot separate the air that did work from the air that leaked. At three in the morning, with the factory shut, everything the compressor produces is escaping. That one reading turns an unknown loss into a repair budget, worked through in [the load that runs when nobody is there](/blog/remote-monitoring/compressed-air-leaks-cost/).
