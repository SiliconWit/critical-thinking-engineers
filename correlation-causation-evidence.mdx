"We fitted the energy saver to the cold room and the next bill was 14% lower." It sounds like proof, and the supplier's invoice is paid. But the next month was also cooler, and a cold room uses less energy in cool weather with or without the device. Two things that move together are correlated. That does not show that one causes the other. This lesson gives you the questions and the simple tests that separate "B happened after A" from "A caused B". #Correlation #Causation #Evidence

## Post Hoc Ergo Propter Hoc

This Latin phrase means "after this, therefore because of this". It names a very old error.

The pattern: you see a problem, you make a change, the problem goes away, and you conclude that the change fixed it. There are three other explanations. The problem may have gone away by itself. Something else may have changed at the same time. Or you did not watch long enough to see the problem return.

### Engineering Examples

<CardGrid>
<Card title="The Mysterious Fix" icon="warning">
"The motor was overheating, so I changed the drive settings. The overheating stopped." Did the settings fix it, or did the motor cool during the ten minutes it stood still while you changed them? Put the old settings back and see whether the overheating returns.
</Card>
<Card title="The Lucky Weekend" icon="warning">
"We serviced the generator on Friday and fuel use fell over the weekend." The site uses less power at weekends. Wait for Monday before you thank the service.
</Card>
<Card title="The Cargo Cult Fix" icon="warning">
"Adding a pull-up resistor on that pin always fixes the noise for me." Does it? Or do you add the resistor at the same time as you tidy the wiring and check every connection?
</Card>
</CardGrid>

The fix needs discipline more than skill: **reverse the change and see whether the problem comes back.** If you cannot reverse it, you know only that the problem stopped after the change.

### A Checklist for Post Hoc Reasoning

Before you conclude that your change fixed the problem, ask:

<Steps>
1. **Can I reverse the change?** If the old state brings the problem back, that is much stronger evidence.
2. **Did I watch long enough?** If a fault came once an hour, ten minutes without it proves nothing.
3. **Did anything else change?** The weather, the load, the operator, the time of day.
4. **Can I produce the problem when I choose?** If you can cause the fault, apply the fix and show that the fault no longer appears, the evidence is solid.
5. **Do I understand the mechanism?** If you cannot explain why the change should work, be careful.
</Steps>

## Confounding Variables

<InArticleAd />


A confounding variable is a third thing that acts on both the supposed cause and the supposed effect, so that they move together although neither acts on the other.

The classic example: ice cream sales and drownings both rise in summer. Ice cream does not cause drowning. Hot weather causes both. Temperature is the confounding variable.

### Confounders in Engineering

| Observed Correlation | Possible Confounder |
|---------------------|-------------------|
| Energy use fell after the "energy saver" was fitted | The weather was cooler in the second month |
| Pumps serviced by contractor A fail less often | Contractor A was given the newer sites |
| Motors with vibration sensors fail more often | Sensors were fitted first to the motors that already gave trouble |
| Boards made on Tuesday have fewer defects | The factory does its maintenance on Monday night |
| Devices with newer software fail less in service | The newer software runs on newer hardware |

When you see a correlation, ask: **"What else could explain this?"** If a third variable could drive both sides, you have found a possible confounder.

The program below makes 200 days of ice cream sales and drowning incidents. Both are calculated from the temperature of the day, plus noise, and neither from the other. The two series are correlated. When the effect of temperature is removed from both, the correlation is gone.

```python title="confounding_variable_demo.py"
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

np.random.seed(42)
n_days = 200

# Temperature is the confounder (in Celsius)
temperature = np.random.normal(loc=25, scale=8, size=n_days)

# Ice cream sales driven by temperature + noise
ice_cream = 50 + 3.0 * temperature + np.random.normal(0, 10, n_days)

# Drowning incidents driven by temperature + noise
drowning = 1.0 + 0.15 * temperature + np.random.normal(0, 1.5, n_days)

# Raw correlation between ice cream and drowning
r_raw, p_raw = stats.pearsonr(ice_cream, drowning)
print(f"Raw correlation (ice cream vs drowning):  r = {r_raw:.3f}, p = {p_raw:.6f}")

# Partial correlation: remove temperature's effect from both
slope_ic, intercept_ic, _, _, _ = stats.linregress(temperature, ice_cream)
slope_dr, intercept_dr, _, _, _ = stats.linregress(temperature, drowning)
residual_ic = ice_cream - (slope_ic * temperature + intercept_ic)
residual_dr = drowning - (slope_dr * temperature + intercept_dr)
r_partial, p_partial = stats.pearsonr(residual_ic, residual_dr)
print(f"Partial correlation (temperature removed): r = {r_partial:.3f}, p = {p_partial:.6f}")

print(f"\nBefore controlling for temperature: clear correlation (r={r_raw:.3f})")
print(f"After controlling for temperature:  correlation vanishes (r={r_partial:.3f})")
print(f"Temperature was the confounder all along.")
```

### How to Deal With Confounders

<Steps>
1. **List the possible confounders.** Before you conclude that A causes B, write down what else could explain the link.
2. **Hold them equal.** If you suspect the weather, compare days of similar weather, or divide the energy by a measure of the weather.
3. **Randomize.** If chance decides which units get the change, the confounders you did not think of are spread evenly between the groups.
4. **Say what you did not control.** "A moves with B, and we have not controlled for C" is an honest sentence. "A causes B" is not, yet.
</Steps>

### A worked case: the saving that was the weather

A cold room is held at 4 °C. An "energy saver" is fitted to its compressor. The meter shows:

| | 30 days before | 30 days after |
|---|---|---|
| Energy used | 4,200 kWh | 3,600 kWh |
| Mean outdoor temperature | 29 °C | 24 °C |

The supplier's sum uses the first row only:

$$
\frac{4{,}200 - 3{,}600}{4{,}200} = 14.3\% \text{ saved}
$$

Now use the second row. Heat enters a cold room through its walls roughly in proportion to the difference between the outdoor temperature and the room temperature. That difference was $29 - 4 = 25$ °C before and $24 - 4 = 20$ °C after. Divide the energy by it:

- Before: $4{,}200 / 25 = 168$ kWh for each degree of difference
- After: $3{,}600 / 20 = 180$ kWh for each degree of difference

At the old rate, the cooler month should have needed only $168 \times 20 = 3{,}360$ kWh. The room used 3,600 kWh, which is 7% more than that, not 14% less. The whole fall in the bill, and more, is explained by the weather.

The proportional rule is rough: door openings and warm stock also add heat. So do not conclude that the device wastes 7%. Conclude that these two bills are no evidence of a saving. A fair test switches the device off and on in alternate weeks and compares the energy for each degree of difference.

## Controlled Experiments: Change One Variable at a Time

<InArticleAd />


The strongest tool for showing a cause is the controlled experiment: hold everything the same except the one thing you want to test.

Suppose a borehole pump stops now and then and you suspect the float switch. Do not replace the float switch, clean the strainer and change the controller settings in the same visit. If the stops end, you will not know which change did it. Replace the float switch only. Watch. If the stops continue, put the old one back and test the next idea.

### The Scientific Method for Engineers

<Card title="One Variable at a Time" icon="rocket">
1. Observe the symptom (the cold room compressor starts 12 times an hour).
2. Form a hypothesis (the thermostat probe sits in the warm air from the door).
3. Design a test that changes one thing only (move the probe to the back wall).
4. Predict the result if the hypothesis is right (far fewer starts).
5. Run the test and observe.
6. If the starts fall, you have evidence, not proof. Move the probe back and confirm that the starts rise again.
</Card>

This feels slow, and you will want to change three things at once to save time. When the problem then remains, or goes away, you will not know which change helped, which did nothing and which added a new problem.

### When You Must Change Multiple Things

Sometimes one change forces another: the new controller needs a different sensor. Then record every change and say plainly what you do not know: "After the controller and the sensor were replaced, the trips stopped. We have not separated the two."

### The Control Group Mindset

A control group is a set of units that does not get the change, kept for comparison. You can use the idea in daily work. Fit the new controller to half of the pumps and leave the other half as they are. Update the software on some sites and watch the others. Without such a baseline you cannot tell whether a result is better, worse or the same.

## A/B Testing: The Engineering Clinical Trial

<InArticleAd />


An A/B test is a controlled experiment on many units at once: two versions run side by side and their results are compared.

### Software A/B Testing

A web page has a current version A and a version B with a larger "Buy" button. Each visitor is sent to A or B at random, half to each, and the clicks are counted for a week. If B gets clearly more clicks, the size of the button matters.

The important words are "at random". If visitors choose their own group, the groups may differ in ways that confound the result.

### Hardware A/B Testing

The same works with equipment. A factory has 20 identical conveyor gearboxes and wants to compare two lubricants. Chance decides which 10 get lubricant A and which 10 get B, and the temperatures are compared over the same weeks. Do not put A on the ten gearboxes near the furnace and B on the ten near the door, and do not measure A in the morning and B in the afternoon.

### What Makes a Good A/B Test

| Requirement | Why |
|-------------|-----|
| Random assignment | Makes the two groups comparable |
| Large enough sample | A small sample gives a noisy result |
| One variable changed | With more, you do not know what caused the difference |
| Length fixed in advance | Running until you "see a difference" biases the result |
| A significance test | Tells you whether the difference is larger than chance gives |

The most common mistake is "peeking": looking at the results every day and stopping as soon as one version is ahead. Chance alone puts one version ahead for a time, even when there is no real difference. Fix the sample size and the length before you start.

Some decisions cannot be tested this way. You cannot build two bridges to see which lasts longer. Then you use calculation, records and expert judgment, and you say that this evidence is weaker than an experiment.

## Anecdotal Evidence vs. Systematic Evidence

<InArticleAd />


"It worked at our other site" is an anecdote: one case, in one place, at one time. It says little about your site. An anecdote is useful as a source of ideas to test. It is not a basis for a decision.

### The Hierarchy of Evidence

A rough order, from weakest to strongest:

<Steps>
1. **Expert opinion.** "I have done this for 20 years and I think this is right." Valuable, but limited by one person's experience and biases.
2. **Anecdotal evidence.** "This worked for me on my last job." One case, which may not be typical.
3. **Case study.** "We recorded in detail what happened at this site." More careful, but still one case.
4. **Observational study.** "Across 50 sites, those with monthly inspections had 30% fewer breakdowns." A correlation over many cases, with confounders not controlled.
5. **Controlled experiment.** "Chance decided which 20 sites got monthly inspections and which 20 did not, and all else was held the same." Much stronger evidence of a cause.
6. **Systematic review.** "We combined the results of 15 controlled experiments." Several independent studies that point the same way.
</Steps>

### Applying This in Practice

When you hear a claim, place it on this list. "A forum post says this pump brand is more efficient" is an anecdote. "We ran both pumps on our own borehole for alternate weeks over three months and pump B used 9% less energy for each cubic metre" is a controlled experiment.

You do not need a systematic review to choose a pump. You do need to know how strong your evidence is, and to be as confident as it allows and no more.

The next program shows what one chosen point can do. The data rise steadily over 24 months, with noise. A person who looks only at one month where the value fell can say "it is going down", against the whole record.

```python title="anecdotal_vs_trend.py"
import numpy as np

np.random.seed(42)
n_months = 24
months = np.arange(n_months)

# Clear upward trend with noise
trend = 100 + 1.5 * months
noise = np.random.normal(0, 5, n_months)
data = trend + noise

# Find a noticeable dip (a point lower than its predecessor)
dips = np.where(np.diff(data) < -3)[0] + 1  # indices where data dropped
cherry_pick = dips[len(dips) // 2]  # pick a dip near the middle

# Full trend: linear regression
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(months, data, 1)
total_change = 100 * (data[-1] - data[0]) / data[0]
local_change = 100 * (data[cherry_pick] - data[cherry_pick - 1]) / data[cherry_pick - 1]

print(f"Full dataset: {n_months} months of measurements")
print(f"Linear trend slope: +{slope:.2f} units/month")
print(f"Overall change (first to last): {total_change:+.1f}%\n")
print(f"Cherry-picked point (month {cherry_pick}): {local_change:+.1f}% change from previous month")
print(f"\nAnecdotal claim: 'See, month {cherry_pick} dropped {abs(local_change):.1f}%! It is declining!'")
print(f"Full trend:       'The data rose {total_change:.1f}% over {n_months} months.'")
print(f"\nOne data point is not a trend. Always look at the full picture.")
```

### The "N=1" Problem

Much technical practice rests on one case: "we tried this at one site and it worked". That is weak evidence for a general rule. It may have worked because of something special at that site, or it may have gone well whatever you had chosen.

When a decision rests on one case, say so: "This worked well at site X, and we have not tested it anywhere else."

<Card title="Calibrating Confidence" icon="information">
A useful habit: after a claim, give your confidence from 1 to 10. "I am about 3 out of 10 sure that this controller handles a power dip correctly" makes your doubt visible, and tells others how much weight to give your advice.
</Card>

## "The Code Works When I Add a Delay"

<InArticleAd />


This is a common case of correlation taken for causation in embedded programs. The program crashes. You add a delay of 10 ms somewhere. The crash goes away, and the product is released.

What probably happened: two parts of the program use the same data at the same time without any rule about who goes first. This is called a race condition. The delay moved the timing enough that the two seldom meet. The fault is hidden, not fixed, and it will return when the timing moves again.

### Other "Fixes" That Hide a Fault

| "Fix" | What It Probably Hides |
|-------|----------------------|
| Adding a print or log statement | The output slows the program enough to avoid a race condition |
| Making a buffer larger | An error at the edge of the buffer that was never handled |
| Restarting the service every night | Memory or another resource that is slowly used up |
| "Switching it off and on fixes it" | A fault in the start-up sequence, or a state that only builds up with time |

If your fix is on this list, keep looking. The real fault is still there.

### How to Tell the Difference

<Tabs>
<TabItem label="Correlation (Masking)">
**Signs that you are hiding a fault:**
- The fix is a delay, a retry or a restart.
- You do not understand why the fix works.
- The problem still returns now and then.
- The fix depends on timing (it works at 10 ms and not at 5 ms).
</TabItem>
<TabItem label="Causation (Fixing)">
**Signs that you have fixed it:**
- You found the cause (for example, two tasks writing one variable).
- You understand why the fix works (for example, a lock now lets only one task in at a time).
- The fix holds whether the system runs fast or slow.
- You can produce the old fault with a test, and the test passes with the fix.
</TabItem>
</Tabs>

## Thinking in Mechanisms

<InArticleAd />


The difference between correlation and causation is often whether you can describe a mechanism: the chain of steps by which A leads to B.

"Cleaning the condenser stopped the high pressure trips" is a statement of correlation. "Dust on the condenser fins blocked the air. The refrigerant could not give up its heat, so its pressure rose until the high pressure switch stopped the compressor. Clean fins let the air through again" is a mechanism. With a mechanism you have much better reason to believe that A caused B.

### Questions That Reveal Mechanisms

When someone claims that A caused B, ask:

- **How?** What is the physical path from A to B?
- **Why this amount?** A real mechanism should explain the size of the effect.
- **What would break it?** There should be conditions in which it does not work.
- **What else does it predict?** A good mechanism predicts things you have not yet looked at. Look at them.

### Building a Causal Diagram

A causal diagram is a drawing of causes and effects. Each variable is a name, and each arrow points from a cause to its effect.

A sensor gives noisy readings whenever a motor runs. There are two possible paths:

```text
            ┌─> electrical noise ────────┐
Motor runs ─┤                            ├─> noisy reading
            └─> vibration ─> loose plug ─┘
```

The drawing makes your assumptions visible. Here it shows two paths from the cause to the effect, and a screened cable would close only the upper one.

## Survivorship Bias

<InArticleAd />


Survivorship bias is a conclusion drawn only from the cases that succeeded. "This supplier's reference sites all run without trouble" tells you about the sites the supplier chose to show. The sites that had trouble are not on the list.

When you see a pattern among successes, ask: "What about the failures?" If the failures show the same pattern, the pattern has nothing to do with success. The lesson on [cognitive biases](/education/critical-thinking-engineers/cognitive-biases-engineering/) works through the best known example, the bullet holes that Abraham Wald studied on returning bombers.

## Simpson's Paradox: When Aggregated Data Lies

<InArticleAd />


Simpson's paradox is a result that holds in every group of the data and reverses when the groups are added together. It sounds impossible, and it is common.

### A Maintenance Example

Two repair teams are compared. Team A is faster on small repairs and faster on large repairs. Yet over all repairs, team B has the lower average time. How?

| Repair | Team A | Team B | Faster |
|-------------|------------|------------|--------|
| Small | 10 h average (20 jobs) | 12 h average (80 jobs) | A |
| Large | 100 h average (80 jobs) | 120 h average (20 jobs) | A |
| Overall average | 82 h | 33.6 h | B appears faster |

The overall averages are $(20 \times 10 + 80 \times 100)/100 = 82$ h for team A and $(80 \times 12 + 20 \times 120)/100 = 33.6$ h for team B. The teams did different mixes of work: A was given mostly large repairs and B mostly small ones. The mix, not the speed, decided the overall average. On an equal mix of jobs, A would win overall as well.

So look at the groups before you trust a total. Compare two contractors, two machines or two processes on the same kind of work, or the comparison only measures who got the easy jobs.

## Bayesian Thinking for Engineers

<InArticleAd />


Bayes' theorem tells you how far to change a belief when new evidence arrives. The idea: ask how likely the evidence is if your hypothesis is true, and how likely it is if your hypothesis is false.

### A Practical Example

A pump trips now and then. You have two hypotheses:

- **Hypothesis A:** Dips in the supply voltage. From experience at this site you give it 60% before any test. This starting value is called the prior probability.
- **Hypothesis B:** A failing float switch, 40%.

You record the trips for a month, and most of them fall in the evening, when the local supply is most heavily loaded. How should this change your belief?

If voltage dips are the cause, an evening pattern is what you would expect: say a 70% chance of seeing it under $H_A$. If the float switch is the cause, trips should come at any hour, and an evening pattern would be chance: say 20% under $H_B$.

The evidence is much more likely under $H_A$, so your belief should move toward $H_A$, but not all the way, because $H_B$ is not excluded. With these numbers:

$$
P(H_A \mid \text{evidence}) = \frac{0.60 \times 0.70}{0.60 \times 0.70 + 0.40 \times 0.20} = \frac{0.42}{0.50} = 0.84
$$

Your belief in the voltage hypothesis rises from 60% to 84%.

In practice you seldom calculate. You ask: **"How likely is this evidence under each hypothesis?"** Evidence that is equally likely under both tells you nothing. Evidence that is much more likely under one of them tells you a lot.

### Pre-Test vs. Post-Test Probability

The belief after the evidence is called the posterior probability. How far it moves from the prior depends on how well the test separates the hypotheses. A test that gives the same result whatever the cause is useless, however technical it looks. Before you choose the next test in fault finding, ask: "Will its result be different under my competing hypotheses?" If not, choose another test.

## Exercises

<InArticleAd />


1. **Spot the fallacy.** A site reports: "After we changed to the new lubricant, bearing failures fell by 40%." List three confounding variables that could explain this without the lubricant.

2. **Design a controlled experiment.** A colleague claims that a monthly inspection prevents more breakdowns than a vibration sensor. Design a test of the claim. What would you hold the same? What would you measure? How would you deal with the age of the machines?

3. **Evaluate evidence strength.** For one product you have a forum post: "this controller is very reliable, we have used it for years". For another you have a published test of 1,000 start and stop cycles on a pump like yours. Which is the stronger evidence, and why?

4. **Find the mechanism.** A sensor gives noisy readings when a motor runs and clean readings when it is off. Propose a mechanism, step by step, and not only "the motor causes noise". Then propose a test that would confirm it or rule it out.

5. **Survivorship bias.** You read that the most successful companies in your industry seldom write detailed requirements. Should you stop writing them? What could survivorship be hiding, and what data would you need?

6. **Bayesian update.** A device fails now and then. You believe there is a 70% chance that the cause is in the hardware and 30% that it is in the software. You run the same software on a second device and the failure does not appear. How should this change your belief? What if the second device is a different hardware revision?

:::tip[Instrument it]
This is the trap that catches every monitoring system. Two sensors rise together and it is tempting to say one caused the other, or that the pattern means a fault, when both may be driven by a third thing: the morning warm-up, the shift change, the load going up. An anomaly detector flags correlation, not cause, so before you [act on an alert](https://siliconwit.io) ask what else changed at that moment. The discipline that separates a real fault from a coincidence is the same one that keeps a monitor trustworthy.
:::

## Practical Takeaways

<InArticleAd />


<Card title="Questions to Ask About Any Claim" icon="list-format">
Before accepting that A caused B, ask these questions:

1. Is this just correlation, or is there a mechanism?
2. What confounders could explain the association?
3. Was a controlled experiment done, or is this observational?
4. How large is the sample? Could it be random variation?
5. Can the result be reproduced independently?
6. What would it look like if the claim were false?
</Card>

None of these questions needs advanced statistics. They need the habit of stopping before you accept an explanation that suits you.

## Summary

<InArticleAd />


- A change that came before an improvement did not necessarily cause it. Reverse the change and see whether the problem returns.
- A third variable, such as the weather or the load, can move two things together. Ask what else changed, and compare like with like.
- A controlled experiment changes one thing and keeps a baseline. It is the most reliable way to show a cause.
- When you cannot run one, say how strong your evidence is, and look for the mechanism: the chain of steps from cause to effect.
